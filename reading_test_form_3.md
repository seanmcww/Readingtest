### Passage: "The Deep End"

Every Tuesday afternoon, Jasmine's class went swimming. For most students, it was the best lesson of the week. For Jasmine, it was torture. While her classmates raced and dived, she stayed in the shallow end, pretending she was practicing her strokes.

The problem was simple: Jasmine couldn't swim in deep water. She'd learned the basic movements in the shallow end, but the moment her feet couldn't touch the bottom, panic would grip her chest like a cold hand. She'd thrash back to where she could stand, heart hammering.

"You're doing the right movements," her instructor Mr. Davies had told her last week. "You just need to trust yourself."

Trust herself? That was easy for him to say, standing safely on the pool deck.

Today felt different, though. As Jasmine climbed out of the pool after another shallow-end session, she noticed Ella sitting on the bench, her ankle wrapped in a bandage.

"What happened?" Jasmine asked, sitting down beside her.

"Twisted it in PE yesterday," Ella sighed. "I'm so gutted. The swimming gala is next month, and I've been training for ages."

Jasmine had forgotten about the gala. Each class could enter four swimmers for the relay race. She'd assumed it didn't involve her.

"Miss Rodriguez asked if you'd take my place," Ella continued. "You'd need to swim one length. In the deep end, obviously."

Jasmine's stomach dropped. "I can't. I mean, I don't swim in deep water."

"Oh." Ella looked genuinely disappointed. "That's a shame. We really need four people, or the whole class can't compete."

Later that evening, Jasmine couldn't stop thinking about it. Her whole class—thirty students—couldn't enter because she was too scared? It seemed unfair. She thought about how Ella had looked, disappointed not just for herself but for everyone.

The next morning, Jasmine arrived at school early. She found Mr. Davies setting up equipment in the gym.

"Could you help me?" she asked. "With swimming in the deep end? I need to be able to do one length by next month."

Mr. Davies smiled. "I can. But Jasmine, you already know how to swim. You just need to discover that for yourself."

They worked together every Tuesday morning before school. Mr. Davies didn't push her into the deep end. Instead, he had her swim in the shallow end but made her practice going underwater, holding her breath, feeling what it was like when her feet didn't touch. Gradually, he moved her closer to where the bottom sloped away.

Three weeks later, Jasmine stood at the deep end's edge for the first time. Mr. Davies was in the water, a few meters away.

"Remember," he said calmly, "you already know how to do this. Your body understands. Just let it."

Jasmine jumped. The water closed over her head, and for one terrifying second, the old panic flared. Then her arms began to move—the familiar strokes she'd practiced hundreds of times. Her legs kicked. And she was swimming, actually swimming, in water where she couldn't touch the bottom.

When she reached Mr. Davies, she was laughing and gasping at the same time. "I did it!"

"You did," he agreed. "Though really, you did it three weeks ago. You just needed to believe it."

### Passage: "Why Lightning Doesn't Strike Twice"

The old saying "lightning never strikes twice in the same place" is demonstrably false. Lightning frequently strikes the same location repeatedly, particularly tall structures like skyscrapers and transmission towers. The Empire State Building in New York is struck roughly 100 times per year. Yet this persistent myth reveals something interesting about how humans understand probability and rare events.

Lightning forms when ice crystals in storm clouds collide, creating an electrical charge. The bottom of the cloud becomes negatively charged, while the ground below develops a positive charge. When the difference becomes large enough, electricity discharges through the air in the brilliant flash we see as lightning. The actual bolt we observe is the return stroke—electricity rushing back up from the ground to the cloud at roughly one-third the speed of light.

The path lightning takes isn't random. It follows the route of least resistance through the air. Tall, pointed objects like trees, buildings, or even people standing in open fields create easier pathways for electricity to reach the ground. This is why these structures get struck repeatedly—they remain the most convenient targets in their area. The Empire State Building's height makes it a natural lightning rod, consistently offering the easiest path between cloud and earth.

Thunder, lightning's acoustic companion, results from the bolt's intense heat. Lightning heats the surrounding air to approximately 30,000 degrees Celsius—five times hotter than the sun's surface. This extreme temperature causes the air to expand explosively, creating the shock wave we hear as thunder. The rumbling quality of thunder occurs because the sound reaches us at different times from different parts of the lightning bolt's path.

The delay between seeing lightning and hearing thunder allows us to estimate distance. Light travels so quickly that we see the flash almost instantaneously. Sound, however, travels much slower—roughly one kilometer every three seconds. By counting the seconds between flash and thunder, then dividing by three, you can calculate your approximate distance from the strike in kilometers.

Understanding lightning's physics helps explain why the "never strikes twice" myth persists. Lightning strikes are dramatic and memorable events. When we experience one, our brains focus on the immediate location and moment, making a repeat strike at that exact spot seem impossibly unlikely. We forget that "the same place" might mean a building, mountain, or tower that presents the same attractive target storm after storm. The saying confuses specific moments with enduring geography. Lightning doesn't remember where it struck—it simply follows the laws of physics, over and over again.

### Passage: "The Market at Dawn"

I arrive at the Marrakech souk before sunrise, when the ancient medina is still half-dreaming. The narrow alleyways, which will later throng with tourists and locals alike, lie nearly empty in the blue pre-dawn light. My footsteps echo against walls that have stood for centuries, and for a brief moment, I could be walking through any era—1920, 1820, perhaps even 1220.

The city reveals itself gradually, like a photograph developing. First comes the bread: the yeasty aroma drifting from bakeries where men slide round loaves into traditional clay ovens. The scent is fundamental, primal—it speaks of sustenance and survival, unchanged across millennia. I follow my nose to a small bakery where the baker, his face illuminated by the oven's glow, hands me a still-warm khobz without a word. We're too early for conversation; we communicate through gesture and shared understanding.

As the sky lightens from indigo to pearl, the market begins its transformation. Metal shutters roll upward with a distinctive rumble that reverberates down the covered passages. Shopkeepers arrange their displays with practiced efficiency: pyramids of spices in impossible shades—saffron yellow, paprika red, cumin brown. The colors are so vivid they seem artificial, though they're anything but. These are the same pigments that colored medieval manuscripts, that stained the robes of sultans, that traveled the ancient trade routes connecting three continents.

The air grows complex with layered scents: mint tea brewing in silver pots, leather being worked in the tanneries, orange blossoms from hidden courtyards. Each smell carries information—time of day, season, proximity to particular quarters of the souk. I've learned to navigate partly by nose, recognizing the sharp bite of the metalworkers' district, the sweet heaviness near the perfume vendors, the earthy richness surrounding the vegetable stalls.

By seven, the transformation is complete. Voices rise in a dozen languages and dialects. Vendors call out their wares in rhythmic Arabic; tourists attempt halting French or English; locals negotiate in rapid Darija. The cacophony shouldn't work—it's chaotic, overlapping, occasionally discordant. Yet somehow it creates a kind of urban symphony, each voice finding its place in the overall composition.

What strikes me most forcefully is the continuity. This same market has operated on this same spot for nearly a thousand years. The goods have changed—plastic toys alongside traditional ceramics, polyester djellabas among handwoven wool—but the fundamental pattern persists. People still gather at dawn to buy and sell, to negotiate and socialize, to participate in commerce that is simultaneously transaction and ritual.

Standing in the growing crowd, warm bread in hand, I feel briefly connected to something larger than myself: the endless human need to gather, to trade, to transform raw materials into objects of use and beauty. The souk isn't frozen in the past—it's a living tradition, adapting while maintaining its essential character. This is what authenticity really means: not resistance to change, but continuity of purpose.
